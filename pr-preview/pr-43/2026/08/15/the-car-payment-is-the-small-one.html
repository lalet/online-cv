The Car Payment Ends. The Rest Doesn't.

August 15, 2026
#personal-finance #canada #budgeting

I built a script that reads every credit card and bank statement I’ve had since 2020 and sorts every dollar into a category. Groceries, streaming subscriptions, the dentist, all of it. I did it mostly to catch subscriptions I’d forgotten to cancel. What actually stood out was the car.

Why it hides

A car doesn’t send one bill. It sends a dozen, from a dozen different places: the gas station, the insurer, the mechanic, the city parking meter, the province’s toll authority, whoever fixed the flat. None of them individually feels like much. None of them arrives labeled “cost of owning a car.” So the total never really forms in your head, you just feel a bit poorer in a way you can’t quite trace.

That’s not really an accident. Postwar North America was built around the assumption that everyone drives, and once housing, jobs, and grocery stores are spaced out on that assumption, a car stops being a purchase and becomes a fixed cost of normal life, the way rent is. Streetsblog quotes a researcher describing suburban residents as “locked in an environment and lifestyle that make dependence of the car a necessity.” You don’t choose to carry this expense so much as inherit it.

What the outside numbers say

Statistics Canada’s most recent Survey of Household Spending puts transportation at 15.8% of total household consumption for 2023, second only to shelter (32.1%) and just barely ahead of food (15.7%) for that spot. That’s a national average across renters, homeowners, transit users, and drivers alike, so it’s already diluted by people who don’t own a car at all.

CAA runs a calculator that’s the standard Canadian reference for this, worth plugging your own numbers into once. What struck me hunting for a baseline is how much published figures disagree, self-reported survey numbers land far lower than “full cost of ownership” calculators that include financing and depreciation. Even researchers can’t settle on one number, which is its own small proof this expense is hard to see clearly.

The clearest Canadian breakdown I found was WealthNorth’s category-by-category estimate for a mid-size sedan: depreciation first by a wide margin, then fuel, then financing, then insurance, then maintenance. Depreciation is the one that never shows up on a bank statement, it’s just the gap between what you paid and what the car is worth now, which is exactly the kind of cost my own numbers below can’t see either.

What my own numbers say

I kept mine to percentages rather than dollar figures, since the totals aren’t the interesting part and I’d rather not scatter them online. This is credit card spending only, not the full household ledger, rent, utilities on direct debit, and anything paid straight from a bank account isn’t in here. That makes it a narrower slice than Statistics Canada’s total-consumption number, and it badly undercounts one specific thing: the vehicle purchase itself, since a down payment and loan payments usually move by bank transfer, not a card. Keep that in mind for what follows, over five years of statements:

  • Car spending came out to 16% of everything that hit those cards, close enough to Statistics Canada’s 15.8% that it’s a fun coincidence, even knowing the two numbers aren’t measuring quite the same thing.
  • On the card alone, the breakdown looks lopsided: petrol 37%, insurance 32%, maintenance 17%, and just 6% for the vehicle purchase. That last number is the misleading one, not because it’s wrong, but because it’s only counting what happened to touch a card. Once you add back what actually left a bank account instead, the purchase stops being the smallest thing on this list and becomes the biggest, at least in the years the loan is still running.
  • The car’s share of total spending didn’t spike once and fade either, it sat in a 13-21% band, year after year. It behaves less like a one-time purchase and more like a second rent payment that also happens to need gas.

Petrol isn’t the only option

Every number above comes from a straight gas engine, since that’s what I drive. Worth asking what changes with a different engine underneath, because the options aren’t variations on one theme, they’re different bets on where the cost shows up. A hybrid (HEV) never plugs in, it just takes load off the gas engine with a small battery. A plug-in hybrid (PHEV) adds a bigger battery for 30-60 km of pure electric range before gas kicks in. A full electric (BEV) skips gas entirely and lives or dies on charging access.

Electric is consistently cheaper to run: ThinkEV’s 2026 breakdown puts EV savings at $6,000-22,000 over five to ten years versus an equivalent gas car, mostly from fuel ($1,750-2,200/year) and maintenance ($400-550/year). Kia Victoria’s comparison puts a full EV around $810 a year to drive against roughly $2,000 for a plug-in hybrid SUV, though both move a lot by province, electricity in Quebec, Manitoba, and BC is cheap and clean, elsewhere it’s neither.

Two things complicate that math. Upfront price parity between EVs and gas cars arrived faster than expected, price cuts and returning federal incentives pulled both to the same average transaction price in early 2026, hybrids now carry the bigger premium. And insurance runs the other direction: EVs in Canada cost about 37% more to insure than gas cars, a gap that’s widening as insurers price in expensive battery repairs and thin claims history.

Incentives are also getting less generous by the year: the federal Electric Vehicle Affordability Program offers up to $5,000 off a BEV starting April 2026, first-come first-served. Quebec’s Roulez Vert rebate has shrunk from $7,000 to $4,000 to $2,000 and ends entirely on December 31, 2026. Check current numbers before assuming last year’s rebate still applies.

Petrol and electric land on almost the same sticker price now, hybrid is the expensive one to buy, then insurance flips the order entirely, petrol cheapest, electric priciest by a wide margin. I’ve gone back and forth on this chart more than anything else in the post, and I still haven’t picked a side. It depends on how long you keep the car, how you drive it, and your own province’s markets. I’m not pretending a bar chart solved it.

What I’m actually buying

None of the above is an argument against owning a car. It’s an argument for looking at the number honestly instead of flinching from it, because once I did, I stopped feeling bad about it.

Car ownership gets talked about like it’s one motive wearing different trim levels. Some people buy for the 0-60 time, some for the badge on the trunk, some just like driving the way other people like cooking, an activity that’s the point of itself. None of that is me, I’ve never once cross-shopped horsepower. For me a car turns “I don’t have time for that” into “sure, I can be there.”

Take the trip I make most weekends, either to Montreal or to Toronto. I checked Google Maps while writing this: driving between the two is 5 hours 13 minutes over 542 km, VIA Rail is 4 hours 50 minutes downtown to downtown, and flying is 1 hour 20 minutes in the air starting around $389. That flight looks like it wins by a mile, until you count the getting to the airport, security, boarding, and the trip from the other airport at the far end. Add the whole journey and all three land in roughly the same few hours. The car is just the only one that leaves the second I’m ready and goes exactly to the door I’m headed for.

That flexibility matters more in the boring weeks than the weekend trips. Classes, a weekly music lesson, martial arts, festivals, a gym habit I actually keep, none of it is remarkable alone, but stacked over a normal week it’s a lot of specific places at specific times. Run that on transit schedules built around commuting hours and half of it quietly stops happening, not from a decision to stop, just friction winning often enough.

So when I look at that 16% again, I don’t read it as money lost, I read it as the price of a week where I decide what happens with my evenings instead of a bus schedule deciding for me. And that’s really the trade-off: time doesn’t have a market rate posted next to the price of gas, so trying to weigh “an evening with people I care about” against a car payment doesn’t work, the two aren’t priced in the same currency. The car isn’t cheap. It’s just not the expense I resent, since there’s no number on the other side of the scale to weigh it against.

The actual point

The number everyone braces for is the purchase price, or the size of the loan payment. That’s the one on the sticker, the one you negotiate, the one you tell your friends about. And once I corrected for how much of it never shows up on a card, it’s not the small one at all, while it’s active, it’s one of the biggest recurring costs a car has.

What makes it different isn’t its size. It’s that it ends. A few years in, the payment drops to zero. Insurance and gas don’t, they keep billing you long after the car is paid off, in amounts too spread out across too many receipts to ever add up to a single scary number, until you sit down and add them up yourself.
